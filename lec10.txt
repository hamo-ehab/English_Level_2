         Vocabulary: Communicating
Argue: to speak angrily to somebody because you disagree with them.
Example: My brothers are always arguing.


Promise: to tell somebody that you will definitely do or not do something, or that
something will definitely happen.
Example: ‘We haven't got time to go to the park.’ ‘But you promised!’


Announce: to tell people something officially, especially about a decision, plans,
etc., announce something.
Example: He officially announced his intention to resign at today's press
conference.
           Vocabulary: Communicating
Inform: to tell somebody about something, especially in an official way.
Example: The government took this decision without consulting Parliament
or informing the public.


Confirm: to state or show that something is definitely true or correct, especially by
providing evidence.
Example: His guilty expression confirmed my suspicions.


Reply: to say or write something as an answer to somebody/something.
Example: He was quick to reply.
           Vocabulary: Communicating
Convince: to make somebody/yourself believe that something is true.
Example: I'd be very surprised if this argument convinces anybody.


Repeat: to say or write something again or more than once.
Example: She repeated the word several times.

           Vocabulary: Spoken Communication
Chat: An informal and friendly talk, typically one that is light and casual in nature. It
can be brief and cover everyday topics.
Example: I had a pleasant chat with my neighbor about her garden.


Conversation: An informal spoken exchange of thoughts, information, or news
between two or more people. It is more substantial and interactive than a chat.
Example: We had a long conversation about our plans for the future.


Discussion: The act of talking about something in a detailed way with two or more
people, often with the purpose of reaching a decision or exploring a topic in depth.
Example: The council held a discussion about the new park regulations.
           Vocabulary: Spoken Communication
Speech: A formal talk delivered to an audience. It is typically a one-way
communication from the speaker to the listeners.
Example: The president gave a speech to the nation on the state of the economy.


Lecture: A formal presentation intended to teach or inform an audience about a
particular subject, often given in an educational setting like a university.
Example: The professor delivered a lecture on ancient Roman history.


Debate: The act of talking about something in a detailed way with two or more
people, often with the purpose of reaching a decision or exploring a topic in depth.
Example: The candidates engaged in a lively debate about tax policy.
           Vocabulary: Spoken Communication
Presentation: A speech or talk in which a new product, idea, or piece of work is
shown and explained to an audience. It often uses visual aids like slides.
Example: She prepared a presentation with charts and graphs to show her findings
to the board.


Have a chat: to have a conversation, usually one that is friendly and informal.
Example: Why don't you give me a call and we'll have a chat?


Join a conversation: To participate in a discussion or dialogue with others.
Example: She decided to join the conversation about the latest book.
          Vocabulary: Spoken Communication

Take part in: to be actively involved in something with other people.
Example: All the children took part in the Thanksgiving play.

    Considering Modern Communication
Advertisements for the latest smartphones, tablets, laptops and other
gadgets say that they will make communication faster and more efficient.
Naturally enough, because new technology can do so many things so easily,
people have come to depend on it. But this has some unfortunate
consequences.

Geoffrey Turnlin is the author of a book on the theme of communication in
our contemporary world. His basic argument – that people today give too
much importance to technology rather than each other – will surprise no
one, but his writing about it is strong and clear. He says the result of this
dependency is that people feel disturbed, especially by the pressure of using
these gadgets to communicate.
      Considering Modern Communication
Electronic devices were certainly designed to make the sending and receiving
of messages faster than it was in the past, but there is no reason why the act of
just hitting the ‘Send’ button should create a message. Real communication is
not shown until the other person understands the message, and people forget
about this important fact. In far too many cases, Turnlin says, adding an extra
minute considering whether or not the message is understood – can make a
person a much more effective communicator. His argument is that most people
are focusing only on efficiency when they approach the majority of their
exchanges. They want to clear their inbox and respond to new text or voice
messages as soon as they come in. Similarly, they are in the habit of getting
face-to-face conversations over as quickly as possible so they can move on to
the next thing. They feel that they have so many communication tasks to deal
with every day that they spend as little time on each one as they can and are
satisfied just by crossing one more job off their to-do list.
      Considering Modern Communication
However, meaningful and effective communication is possible only when
the speaker or writer thinks about their goals. “In contrast with average
communicators, great communicators think carefully about what they
would like to achieve rather than about what they would like to say,”
Turnlin says. He makes the point that it can be very difficult for people to
stop themselves from saying something, especially when they are feeling
angry or upset. By making it so easy for everyone to share their thoughts
and feelings, technology has encouraged them to do so. But these same
emotional messages, unfortunately, are also the ones that stop them from
achieving their communication goals.
     Considering Modern Communication
One of the greatest problems with the digital age is that sending a message to
lots of people is just as easy as sending a message to one person. But having a
conversation with someone much older or younger than you may be different
from speaking to someone your age. Similarly, a conversation with one friend
is different from a discussion with 100 friends. But because all people have to
do is click a button to send a message to all of their contacts or to post a social
media message for the world to see, they think differently, as adding people to
a message doesn’t really require any extra thought. Turnlin argues that
communication gets much more difficult as the number of people a message is
sent to is increased. Including an unintended audience means there are more
points of view to take into account. When people do not consider the
perspective of the recipient, it may mean that the other person does not
understand what was said or, more seriously perhaps, might be offended by
the message. So before someone sends a message to a group, he or she
    Considering Modern Communication
should ask questions like, “What does my uncle think about this post?” or
“I wonder how this email is going to come across to the other departments
in my workplace.” Adding people complicates communication and when
that is forgotten, misunderstandings will follow.

People now just use their devices without pausing to think. But these new
tools do not automatically copy all the features that made earlier
communication effective. Eventually, people will remember that these
devices have a remarkable potential only when used with thoughtful
human input. If people are not in too much of a hurry to press ‘Send’, they
will be able to take full advantage of all the amazing opportunities for
meaningful communication that the digital age can offer.
